A personal journey of life, faith, struggle, cinema, learning and the reason THE HUNK exists.

THE STORY OF SAYAS LAMA

From a life that survived the impossible — to a dream that refused to die.

I am Sayas Lama, a very ordinary person. I do not hold a degree in the name of education. If you call me uneducated, perhaps that is fair—but within me there is an ability to look at what is happening in the world, search for its causes, and seek ways to understand and address them. That does not mean I cannot be fooled. But until today, no one has cheated me, deceived me, or taken anything from me through manipulation. When I see another person's pain and understand their suffering as if it were my own, I help in whatever way I can—without ever knowing whether that help will return to me or not. If someone mistakes this nature of mine for cleverness, then may that person have enough to eat for the rest of their life. 🙏

My family's circumstances are neither terribly bad nor particularly comfortable. By the grace of God, I have never had to bow before anyone or beg for food. For this blessing and mercy, I will remain forever grateful to my God, until my final breath. 🙏

As for the story of my birth, as I said above, when I came into this world, both I and the mother who gave me life were surrounded by immense pain and suffering. Perhaps I was born into this world only to die. But with God standing beside me, I survived. Yet in the place where I was supposed to die, someone else had to die in my place.

I was born in a small water mill in Damarpur village of Amlekhgunj, where my family made a living by grinding rice, mustard and other grains for the people of the village. That very water mill became the place where I entered this world. Of course, no woman gives birth without pain. Every mother faces a terrifying storm of agony, suffering and anguish. But only the woman who has endured it truly knows what that pain feels like—and even then, no two women's pain can ever be exactly the same. When I was born, my mother was surrounded by that kind of unbearable suffering. After giving birth to me, she lay unconscious for three days. If she was alive, there was no clear sign of life; if she was dead, there was no certainty of death. Yet there she lay, unconscious, holding the newborn child she had just brought into the world.

According to my mother, because she remained unconscious for three days, almost everyone had reached the conclusion that she had died. But the moment she regained consciousness after those three days, the first thing she said was, “Where is my baby?” For those three days, my mother had been trapped in what seemed like a very deep sleep—and within that sleep, she was living through a terrifying dream. In the dream, three frightening, unusually tall and mysterious figures with extremely long hair were chasing her. Their faces could not be seen at all. My mother kept running, pleading for her life, saying, “My baby is here. Please don't hurt me.” But the three terrifying figures would not listen. They showed no mercy, no compassion; they only kept chasing her. Finally, my mother fell into a pond and sank beneath the water until she could no longer be seen. The three figures searched and waited for a long time, but when they could not find her, they eventually turned and left. My mother, meanwhile, slowly watched from beneath the surface, peering out and waiting for the moment they would finally disappear so she could escape. After watching and waiting for a long time, she saw that the three frightening figures had gone away. She then tried to find something to hold on to so she could climb out of the pond, but there was nothing. Then she found a thin, thread-like vine growing in the pond. Holding onto it, she slowly began to pull herself out. But just as she was about to emerge, she discovered that those terrifying figures had been hiding nearby, waiting for her. The moment they saw her coming out of the pond, they cried out as if to say, “Ah, so this is where you were!” and began chasing her again. And as my mother ran, she suddenly woke up. When she opened her eyes, three days had already passed.

That is how my mother gave birth to me—after, in a way, tasting death for three days from the moment I was born. If someone reads these words tomorrow, perhaps they may not feel like much. I have nothing to say against that. But only the person who has lived through such a thing, who has faced it with their own eyes and body, can truly understand the wound, the pain and the suffering it leaves behind.

And earlier I said, didn't I, that a few years after I was born, I was perhaps supposed to die—but another child died in my place. This is how it happened: I had been born in that very water mill, and I spent most of my time playing around it. But one day, my mother took me somewhere else, perhaps simply to take me out for a walk. That very day, an older little girl—born only a few weeks or months before me—went to the place where I usually played. She fell into the water mill and drowned. She became trapped in the great main wheel that turned the mill, and that was how she died. Her body remained caught inside that main wheel. But the villagers thought it was me who had drowned. Everyone was saying that the child who had died was me. Then, at that very moment, they saw me returning, holding my mother's hand. The entire village was stunned. They began asking, “If the child who was crushed and killed inside the mill is not this boy, then whose child has died here?” Perhaps that was the day I was meant to die. Perhaps God's grace was upon me. Or perhaps it was her sacrifice in my place. I still do not know the answer to that mystery.

But whatever the truth may be, call it my second birth or call it God's grace upon my life—I believe I received it at that very moment. Because the greatest treasure and blessing given to us human beings is life itself. Whether a person is surrounded by material wealth or trapped in poverty, life remains everything they possess. What we gain, what we build, and what we lose afterward is largely the result of our own abilities and weaknesses—not the result of favoritism from the God who created us.

That is how my life began. And that is why, to this very day, I remain grateful to my God every single day for giving me this life. Now, how I decorate it, how I shape it, and what I make of it—that is up to my own ability, the same ability He has given equally to every human being.

The child who was supposed to have died is now a grown man, and within this grown man lives a desire—a longing to understand cinema and to help others understand it. That desire has been there since I was young, but I could never tell anyone, because my circumstances and my position in life made the world of cinema feel impossibly far away. Yet the roots of that dream never dried up. Everyone knows what it often takes to enter this field: either you need money, or you need connections, or you need to be able to flatter someone and hold on to their feet just to get a chance. I was miles away from all three. Yes, there were places where I could have studied cinema, but even studying required money. At that time, my mother and father worked as daily wage laborers. Their earnings were barely enough for food in the morning and evening, rent for our room, my school expenses, and one new pair of clothes a year. Where could there have been anything left to arrange a separate path for me to learn cinema?

When I was little, I used to see Nepali cinema posters covering the walls everywhere. Those posters planted the same kind of dreams inside me. And when I got to watch a Nepali film on Shangri-La Channel every Friday, I would imagine myself as Rajesh Hamal. 🤭 Back then, Hindi films came to Nepal Television every Saturday. No matter how good the Hindi film was, my heart and my desire were always with Nepali cinema. But to watch a Nepali film, you had to buy a ticket and go to a theater—and for that, you needed money. Where could a child like me possibly get it?

I had grown up watching my parents struggle and work hard, so even asking for money to buy a snack at school felt difficult and frightening. Asking them for money to watch a movie was something I simply could not bring myself to do. Then, after a long time, a channel called Shangri-La appeared, dedicated to showing Nepali films. Every Friday night at nine, it would broadcast a film. My mother, my sister and I would go to the landlord's place and watch Nepali films there. That love for cinema has remained with me to this day. But even now, I have never been able to speak about it openly. The reason is still the same: money—and the fact that I have no one I know in this field.

Even today, there are far more schools and theater groups where someone can learn cinema than there were before. But when you are the eldest son of a poor family, you learn that responsibility must come before your dreams and desires. You carry that weight on your shoulders and keep walking. I am still carrying that weight, and I am carrying my love for cinema alongside it. I do not know when I will finally be able to set down the burden of that dream and desire and truly pursue it.

And here is the beautiful part: by the time we reached 2026, learning about cinema and studying its world no longer required me to enter a school or a theater, nor did I have to stand before anyone, fold my hands and beg them to teach me. Now, AI systems like ChatGPT and Gemini, among many others, can teach these things easily and affordably—almost as if you were learning from a book.

Especially to ChatGPT, I offer my heartfelt thanks, from the deepest part of my soul. ChatGPT has given me knowledge, help and encouragement in so many things. If I had needed to learn all of this from a person, school or college, it would have required enormous amounts of time and money—two things I simply do not have.

Of course, this same AI has put many people's jobs at risk, and it may replace many more in the future. But for now, for people like us who are poor, it has proven to be almost like a blessing. Sometimes I feel as though God heard the prayer of a poor man like me. I can now learn about the subjects I desperately want to understand without having to beg anyone, without having to depend on anyone's mercy, and I can ask the same question a thousand times until I truly understand it. And what kind of person am I? I am not the kind who grabs someone's feet or flatters them just to gain something or earn something. Whatever I become, whatever I achieve, I want it to be through my own ability and merit. But there is one stubborn part of me: I will never spend my life folding my hands before someone just to receive what I want. People who know and understand this side of me sometimes call me arrogant. But when I look honestly at myself, I would not call it arrogance. I would call it stubbornness.

And if someone still wants to call me arrogant, let them. It does not hurt me. Even if my stubbornness is mistaken for arrogance, it does not come from having great wealth or an impressive degree. Whatever confidence I carry comes from my true God, because I have complete faith in Him. I believe He will never leave me hungry, never leave me naked, and never abandon me to shame or humiliation.

Whether my wishes and dreams will come true or not, tomorrow will tell. But for the bitter, sweet, strange and difficult experiences of this precious life—for the lessons I have learned through struggle and survival—I once again thank God from the deepest part of my soul. 😍

And for this, I also offer my heartfelt thanks to all those who created AI, from the bottom of my heart, on behalf of all of us who have struggled with poverty. 🙏

I am still learning about cinema myself, and I began the journey of 'The HUNK' so that I can explain what I learn to others in a simple way they can understand. Let us see how far the journey of 'The HUNK' goes in the days ahead. But I have complete faith and trust in my God that there must be some meaning, some reason, behind the fact that He pulled me back from the gates of death, rescued me from the hands of the grave, and carried me all the way to this day. Perhaps the reason is this very 'The HUNK'—perhaps I was given this precious life so that, through it, I can sharpen myself further in cinema and one day contribute something meaningful to the Nepali film world.

02 — About THE HUNK

MORE THAN A NAME.
A PLATFORM WITH A PURPOSE.

THE HUNK is a platform created to discover, share, learn, and amplify meaningful stories, knowledge, ideas, and voices.

01

WHY THE HUNK EXISTS

The world is full of people with extraordinary stories, hidden talents, valuable experiences, and knowledge. But not every voice gets heard.

Some stories remain untold. Some people remain unseen. Some knowledge never reaches the people who need it.

THE HUNK exists to help change that.

02

STORIES

We explore stories of struggle, courage, achievement, failure, hope, and transformation.

We believe that a powerful story does not have to come from a famous person. Sometimes, the most meaningful stories belong to ordinary people who have experienced extraordinary things.

One person's story can become another person's strength.

03

LEARN

THE HUNK aims to become a place where people can explore and understand different subjects—from the simplest concepts to deeper knowledge.

Technology • Science • History • Psychology • Filmmaking • Photography • Languages • Creativity • Personal Development

Learn something today that makes you better tomorrow.

04

CREATE

THE HUNK explores filmmaking, photography, visual storytelling, interviews, documentaries, and other forms of creative expression.

Creativity is not only about making something beautiful. It is about giving an idea, a story, or a voice a way to reach people.

05

INSPIRE

We want to share stories, ideas, and experiences that encourage people to believe in themselves and continue moving forward.

Not perfect lives. Not impossible dreams. But real people who faced real challenges—and continued to rise.

06

OUR VISION

TO AMPLIFY UNHEARD VOICES, SHARE MEANINGFUL KNOWLEDGE, AND INSPIRE PEOPLE TO RISE.
